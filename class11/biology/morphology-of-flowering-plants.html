Morphology of Flowering Plants

Complete notes on root, stem, leaf, inflorescence, flower, fruit, seed and important plant families.

📖 CHAPTER OVERVIEW

Morphology of Flowering Plants

Morphology deals with the study of the external form and structure of plants.

In flowering plants, the main vegetative organs are root, stem and leaf, while reproductive structures include flowers, fruits and seeds.

🌱 PART 1

Root

Main Functions

  • Anchors the plant in the soil.
  • Absorbs water and minerals.
  • Stores food in some plants.
  • Helps in conduction of absorbed water and minerals.

Types of Root Systems

  • Tap root system: A main root develops from the radicle. Common in dicot plants.
  • Fibrous root system: A cluster of similar-sized roots develops. Common in monocots.
  • Adventitious roots: Roots arise from parts other than the radicle.
🔬 ROOT STRUCTURE

Regions of Root

Root Cap

Protects the tender growing tip of the root.

Meristematic Region

Contains actively dividing cells responsible for root growth.

Region of Elongation

Cells increase in length and help the root grow further into the soil.

Region of Maturation

Cells differentiate and root hairs develop.

🌿 MODIFICATIONS

Root Modifications

Storage

Roots store food in plants such as carrot, turnip and sweet potato.

Support

Prop roots and stilt roots provide additional mechanical support.

Respiration

Pneumatophores help plants growing in oxygen-poor soils.

🌿 PART 2

Stem

Characteristics

  • Develops from the plumule of the embryo.
  • Usually grows upward.
  • Has nodes and internodes.
  • Leaves and branches arise at nodes.
  • Terminal or axillary buds may be present.

Functions

  • Supports leaves, flowers and fruits.
  • Conducts water and minerals.
  • Transports prepared food.
  • May store food.
  • Some stems perform photosynthesis.
🌿 MODIFICATIONS

Stem Modifications

Underground Stem

Examples include potato, ginger, turmeric, onion and Colocasia.

Sub-aerial Stem

Some stems spread along the ground or produce new plants from nodes.

Stem Tendrils

Modified stems may help plants climb.

Thorns

Modified stems may provide protection.

🍃 PART 3

Leaf

The leaf is a lateral, generally flattened structure borne at the node of a stem.

Main Functions

  • Photosynthesis.
  • Transpiration.
  • Exchange of gases.

Main Parts

  • Leaf base
  • Petiole
  • Lamina
🍃 LEAF

Venation

Reticulate Venation

Veins form a network. Commonly seen in dicot leaves.

Parallel Venation

Veins run approximately parallel to one another. Commonly seen in monocot leaves.

🍃 LEAF ARRANGEMENT

Phyllotaxy

Alternate

One leaf arises at each node.

Opposite

Two leaves arise at each node.

Whorled

More than two leaves arise at a node.

🌿 MODIFICATIONS

Leaf Modifications

Leaf Tendrils

Help climbing plants attach to support. Example: Pea.

Spines

Modified leaves may protect plants. Example: Cactus.

Storage Leaves

Leaves may become fleshy and store food or water.

🌼 PART 4

Inflorescence

Inflorescence is the arrangement of flowers on the floral axis.

Racemose Inflorescence

The main axis continues to grow and flowers are generally arranged in an acropetal sequence.

Cymose Inflorescence

The main axis terminates in a flower and the flowering sequence is generally basipetal.

🌸 PART 5

Flower

A flower is a modified shoot that functions as the reproductive structure of angiosperms.

Four Main Floral Whorls

  • Calyx: made up of sepals.
  • Corolla: made up of petals.
  • Androecium: male reproductive whorl.
  • Gynoecium: female reproductive whorl.
🌼 MALE REPRODUCTIVE PART

Androecium

Androecium is the male reproductive part of a flower.

  • It consists of stamens.
  • Each stamen generally has a filament and anther.
  • Anther produces pollen grains.
  • Pollen grains carry the male gametophytic cells.
🌸 FEMALE REPRODUCTIVE PART

Gynoecium

Gynoecium is the female reproductive part of a flower.

  • Made up of one or more carpels.
  • Each carpel has stigma, style and ovary.
  • Ovary contains ovules.
  • Ovules develop into seeds after fertilisation.
  • Ovary develops into fruit.
🌸 IMPORTANT

Aestivation

Valvate

Margins of sepals or petals touch one another without overlapping.

Twisted

One margin overlaps the next and the other margin is overlapped.

Imbricate

Margins overlap one another but not in a particular direction.

Vexillary

Characteristic arrangement found in many papilionaceous flowers.

🌱 IMPORTANT

Placentation

Marginal

Placenta forms a ridge along the ventral suture. Example: Pea.

Axile

Ovules develop on the central axis.

Parietal

Ovules develop on the inner wall or peripheral part of the ovary.

Free Central

Ovules develop on a central axis without septa.

Basal

Placenta develops at the base of the ovary.

🍎 PART 6

Fruit

Fruit is generally a ripened ovary developed after fertilisation.

  • Fruits developing from the ovary are called true fruits.
  • In some plants, parts other than the ovary also contribute to fruit formation.
  • Such fruits are called false or accessory fruits.
🌱 PART 7

Seed

A seed is a mature ovule formed after fertilisation.

Main Parts

  • Seed coat
  • Embryo
  • Cotyledons
  • Endosperm in many seeds

Monocot Seed

Typically has one cotyledon.

Dicot Seed

Typically has two cotyledons.

📊 COMPARISON

Monocot vs Dicot

Feature Monocot Dicot
Cotyledons One Two
Root System Usually fibrous Usually tap root
Leaf Venation Usually parallel Usually reticulate
Floral Parts Usually in multiples of three Usually in multiples of four or five
🌼 IMPORTANT FAMILIES

Major Angiosperm Families

Fabaceae

Common examples include pea, gram and bean. Papilionaceous corolla is characteristic of many members.

Solanaceae

Includes potato, tomato, chilli and tobacco.

Liliaceae

Includes lily and related monocot plants.

⚡ QUICK REVISION

Remember These Points

  • Root → anchorage and absorption.
  • Stem → support and conduction.
  • Leaf → photosynthesis and transpiration.
  • Flower → reproductive structure of angiosperms.
  • Androecium → male reproductive whorl.
  • Gynoecium → female reproductive whorl.
  • Ovule → develops into seed.
  • Ovary → develops into fruit.
  • Monocot → one cotyledon.
  • Dicot → two cotyledons.
📝 PRACTICE

Important Questions

Q1. What is morphology?

Morphology is the study of the external form and structure of organisms.

Q2. What are the main parts of a leaf?

Leaf base, petiole and lamina.

Q3. What are the four floral whorls?

Calyx, corolla, androecium and gynoecium.

Q4. What does an ovule develop into?

The ovule develops into a seed after fertilisation.

Q5. What does the ovary develop into?

The ovary generally develops into a fruit.
