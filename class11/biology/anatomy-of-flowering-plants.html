Anatomy of Flowering Plants

Complete notes on plant tissues, tissue systems, root, stem, leaf anatomy and secondary growth.

📖 CHAPTER OVERVIEW

Anatomy of Flowering Plants

Anatomy is the study of the internal structure of plants. Flowering plants have different types of tissues arranged into tissue systems.

The three main tissue systems are the epidermal, ground and vascular tissue systems.

🔬 PART 1

Plant Tissues

Meristematic Tissue

Consists of actively dividing cells responsible for plant growth.

Permanent Tissue

Cells that have lost the ability to divide and become specialised for particular functions.

🌱 MERISTEMATIC TISSUE

Types of Meristems

Apical Meristem

Present at the tips of roots and shoots. Responsible mainly for increase in length.

Intercalary Meristem

Present between mature tissues, commonly near nodes or at the base of internodes in grasses.

Lateral Meristem

Includes vascular cambium and cork cambium. It contributes to increase in girth.

🌿 PERMANENT TISSUE

Simple Permanent Tissues

Parenchyma

Living cells with relatively thin walls. They commonly perform storage and other basic functions.

Collenchyma

Living cells with unevenly thickened walls. Provides support and flexibility to growing parts.

Sclerenchyma

Usually consists of dead cells with thick, lignified walls and provides mechanical strength.

🚰 CONDUCTING TISSUES

Complex Permanent Tissues

Xylem

Conducts water and minerals mainly from roots towards aerial parts.

  • Tracheids
  • Vessels
  • Xylem fibres
  • Xylem parenchyma

Phloem

Transports organic food substances from photosynthetic or storage regions to other parts.

  • Sieve tube elements
  • Companion cells
  • Phloem parenchyma
  • Phloem fibres
🌱 PART 2

Tissue Systems

Epidermal Tissue System

Includes epidermis, stomata and epidermal appendages such as trichomes and root hairs.

Ground Tissue System

Includes tissues other than epidermal and vascular tissues.

Vascular Tissue System

Consists mainly of xylem and phloem arranged into vascular bundles.

🌱 ROOT ANATOMY

Internal Structure of Dicot Root

1. Epiblema

The outermost layer of the root, commonly bearing root hairs.

2. Cortex

Made mainly of parenchymatous cells and lies inside the epiblema.

3. Endodermis

The innermost layer of cortex. Casparian strips occur in its radial and transverse walls.

4. Pericycle

Lies just inside the endodermis and contributes to formation of lateral roots and cambium during secondary growth.

5. Vascular Tissue

Xylem and phloem are arranged radially. Xylem is typically exarch.

6. Pith

Usually small in a dicot root.

🌱 ROOT ANATOMY

Monocot Root

  • Epiblema is the outermost layer.
  • Large parenchymatous cortex is present.
  • Endodermis is distinct.
  • Pericycle lies below the endodermis.
  • Xylem is exarch.
  • Many xylem groups are usually present.
  • Pith is large and well developed.
  • Secondary growth is generally absent.
🌿 STEM ANATOMY

Internal Structure of Dicot Stem

Epidermis

Single-layered outer covering, generally with a cuticle.

Cortex

Differentiated into tissues such as hypodermis, cortical layers and endodermis.

Pericycle

Often present as patches of sclerenchymatous tissue outside the vascular bundles.

Vascular Bundles

Arranged in a ring. They are conjoint, collateral and open because cambium is present.

Medullary Rays

Parenchymatous regions between vascular bundles.

Pith

Large and well developed central region.

🌿 STEM ANATOMY

Internal Structure of Monocot Stem

  • Outer epidermis is covered by a cuticle.
  • Hypodermis is generally sclerenchymatous.
  • Ground tissue is not clearly differentiated into cortex and pith.
  • Vascular bundles are scattered in the ground tissue.
  • Vascular bundles are generally closed.
  • Protoxylem lacuna may be present.
🍃 LEAF ANATOMY

Dorsiventral Leaf

A typical dicot leaf is dorsiventral, meaning its upper and lower surfaces differ in structure.

  • Upper epidermis is generally single layered.
  • Mesophyll is differentiated into palisade and spongy tissues.
  • Stomata are commonly more numerous on the lower epidermis.
  • Vascular bundles are surrounded by a bundle sheath.
🍃 LEAF ANATOMY

Isobilateral Leaf

Monocot leaves are generally isobilateral and have similar upper and lower surfaces.

  • Mesophyll is usually not differentiated into palisade and spongy tissues.
  • Stomata may occur on both surfaces.
  • Large specialised cells called bulliform cells may occur on the upper epidermis.
  • Vascular bundles are surrounded by bundle sheath.
🚰 IMPORTANT

Vascular Bundles

Radial

Xylem and phloem occur on different radii. Common in roots.

Conjoint

Xylem and phloem occur together in the same vascular bundle. Common in stems and leaves.

Open Bundle

Cambium is present between xylem and phloem.

Closed Bundle

Cambium is absent between xylem and phloem.

🌳 SECONDARY GROWTH

Secondary Growth

Secondary growth refers mainly to the increase in girth of stems and roots due to activity of lateral meristems.

Vascular Cambium

Cambium produces secondary xylem towards the inside and secondary phloem towards the outside.

Cork Cambium

Cork cambium produces cork or phellem towards the outside and secondary cortex or phelloderm towards the inside.

Annual Rings

Seasonal differences in secondary xylem formation may produce visible growth rings in woody stems.

🌳 WOOD

Heartwood and Sapwood

Heartwood

Older, inner secondary xylem that is generally darker and provides mechanical support.

Sapwood

Younger, outer secondary xylem that is involved mainly in conduction of water and minerals.

📊 COMPARISON

Monocot vs Dicot Anatomy

Feature Dicot Monocot
Root xylem Usually fewer groups Usually many groups
Root pith Usually small Large
Stem vascular bundles Generally arranged in a ring Generally scattered
Stem cambium Present in typical open bundles Generally absent in closed bundles
Leaf Usually dorsiventral Usually isobilateral
⚡ QUICK REVISION

Remember These Points

  • Apical meristem → increase in length.
  • Lateral meristem → increase in girth.
  • Xylem → mainly water and mineral conduction.
  • Phloem → food transport.
  • Dicot root → exarch xylem.
  • Monocot root → large pith.
  • Dicot stem → vascular bundles generally in a ring.
  • Monocot stem → vascular bundles generally scattered.
  • Dicot leaf → dorsiventral.
  • Monocot leaf → usually isobilateral.
  • Vascular cambium → secondary growth.
  • Cork cambium → periderm formation.
📝 PRACTICE

Important Questions

Q1. What is anatomy?

Anatomy is the study of the internal structure of organisms.

Q2. Name the three tissue systems.

Epidermal, ground and vascular tissue systems.

Q3. What is the function of xylem?

Xylem mainly conducts water and minerals from roots towards aerial parts.

Q4. What is secondary growth?

It is the increase in girth caused mainly by lateral meristem activity.

Q5. What is the difference between open and closed vascular bundles?

Open bundles have cambium between xylem and phloem, while closed bundles lack cambium.
