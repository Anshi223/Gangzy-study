🦴 Locomotion and Movement

Complete notes covering types of movement, human skeletal system, joints, muscles, muscle contraction, sliding filament theory and important disorders.

📖 Chapter Overview

Movement is a characteristic feature of living organisms. Locomotion is the movement of an organism from one place to another. In humans, movement and locomotion involve the coordinated action of muscles, bones and joints.

Movement + Muscles + Bones + Joints → Locomotion

🔄 1. Types of Body Movement

Different types of movements occur in living organisms and within the human body.

Movement Example
Amoeboid movement Movement of leucocytes
Ciliary movement Movement of cilia in respiratory tract
Muscular movement Walking, running and movement of body parts

🦴 2. Human Skeletal System

The human skeletal system provides support, protection and a framework for movement.

Major functions

  • Provides support to the body.
  • Protects internal organs.
  • Provides attachment sites for muscles.
  • Helps in locomotion.
  • Stores minerals such as calcium and phosphorus.
  • Bone marrow is involved in blood cell formation.

🦴 3. Axial and Appendicular Skeleton

Division Main Components
Axial skeleton Skull, vertebral column, ribs and sternum
Appendicular skeleton Limbs and their girdles

💀 4. Skull

The skull forms the bony framework of the head and protects the brain.

It consists of cranial and facial bones.

Skull → Protection of brain + Framework of face

🦴 5. Vertebral Column

The vertebral column forms the main supporting axis of the body and protects the spinal cord.

It consists of a series of vertebrae arranged in different regions.

Region Number of Vertebrae
Cervical 7
Thoracic 12
Lumbar 5
Sacral 5 fused
Coccygeal Usually 4 fused
Total vertebral segments = 26 bones in the adult vertebral column because several vertebrae are fused.

🫁 6. Thoracic Cage

The thoracic cage is formed mainly by thoracic vertebrae, ribs and sternum.

  • Protects the heart and lungs.
  • Provides attachment for respiratory muscles.
  • Participates in movements associated with breathing.

💪 7. Pectoral and Pelvic Girdles

Pectoral girdle

The pectoral girdle connects the upper limbs to the axial skeleton.

Pelvic girdle

The pelvic girdle connects the lower limbs to the axial skeleton and provides strong support for the body.

🔗 9. Joints

A joint is the point of contact between two bones or between a bone and another skeletal structure.

Major types

  • Fibrous joints
  • Cartilaginous joints
  • Synovial joints

🔒 10. Fibrous Joints

Fibrous joints are generally immovable and are connected by fibrous connective tissue.

Example: Sutures of the skull

🧩 11. Cartilaginous Joints

Cartilaginous joints allow limited movement and are connected by cartilage.

Example: Joints between adjacent vertebrae

🔄 12. Synovial Joints

Synovial joints are freely movable joints containing a synovial cavity.

Joint Example
Ball and socket Shoulder, hip
Hinge Elbow, knee
Pivot Joint involved in rotation of head
Gliding Some joints of wrist and ankle

💪 13. Muscles

Muscles are specialised tissues capable of contraction. They generate force and produce movement.

Three major types

  • Skeletal muscle
  • Smooth muscle
  • Cardiac muscle

🏃 14. Skeletal Muscle

Skeletal muscles are generally attached to bones and are responsible for voluntary movements and locomotion.

  • Striated appearance
  • Usually multinucleate fibres
  • Under voluntary control
  • Attached to bones by tendons

🫀 15. Cardiac Muscle

Cardiac muscle forms the muscular wall of the heart.

  • Striated
  • Involuntary
  • Branched fibres
  • Intercalated discs are present

🧘 16. Smooth Muscle

Smooth muscles occur mainly in the walls of internal organs and blood vessels.

  • Non-striated
  • Involuntary
  • Usually spindle-shaped

🧬 17. Structure of Skeletal Muscle

A skeletal muscle consists of bundles of muscle fibres. Each muscle fibre contains many myofibrils.

Myofibrils contain repeating functional units called sarcomeres.

Muscle → Muscle fibre → Myofibril → Sarcomere

🔬 18. Sarcomere

The sarcomere is the basic functional unit of a striated muscle fibre.

Major proteins

  • Actin — thin filament
  • Myosin — thick filament

Other proteins such as troponin and tropomyosin help regulate muscle contraction.

⚡ 19. Muscle Contraction

Muscle contraction occurs through interaction between actin and myosin filaments.

The process is explained by the sliding filament theory.

Actin slides over myosin → Sarcomere shortens → Muscle contracts

🔄 20. Sliding Filament Theory

  1. A nerve signal reaches the muscle fibre.
  2. Calcium ions are released inside the muscle fibre.
  3. Calcium interacts with the regulatory proteins.
  4. Myosin heads bind to actin.
  5. Myosin heads perform power strokes.
  6. Actin filaments slide towards the centre of the sarcomere.
  7. The sarcomere shortens.
  8. ATP is required for the cycling of myosin heads.

🧪 21. Role of Calcium

Calcium ions play an important role in initiating skeletal muscle contraction.

Ca²⁺ binds to troponin → Tropomyosin moves away from actin's active sites → Cross-bridge formation can occur

🔋 22. Role of ATP

ATP provides energy required for the cross-bridge cycle during muscle contraction.

  • Helps detach myosin from actin.
  • Provides energy for myosin head movement.
  • Supports the cycling of cross-bridges.

🧠 23. Neuromuscular Junction

The neuromuscular junction is the functional connection between a motor neuron and a skeletal muscle fibre.

A neurotransmitter released from the motor neuron initiates events leading to muscle contraction.

📊 24. Skeletal vs Smooth vs Cardiac Muscle

Feature Skeletal Smooth Cardiac
Striations Present Absent Present
Control Voluntary Involuntary Involuntary
Location Attached to bones Internal organs Heart
Special feature Long fibres Spindle-shaped cells Intercalated discs

⚠️ 25. Disorders of Muscular and Skeletal System

Myasthenia Gravis

An autoimmune disorder affecting neuromuscular transmission and causing muscle weakness.

Muscular Dystrophy

A group of genetic disorders characterised by progressive weakness and degeneration of skeletal muscles.

Arthritis

Arthritis refers to inflammation or other disorders affecting joints and may cause pain and restricted movement.

Osteoporosis

Osteoporosis is characterised by reduced bone mass and increased susceptibility to fractures.

Gout

Gout is associated with deposition of uric acid crystals in joints, causing inflammation and pain.

⚡ Quick Revision

  • Locomotion is movement of an organism from one place to another.
  • The skeletal system provides support and protection.
  • Axial skeleton forms the central framework of the body.
  • Appendicular skeleton includes limbs and girdles.
  • Joints connect bones and allow different degrees of movement.
  • Synovial joints are freely movable.
  • Skeletal muscles are generally voluntary and striated.
  • Smooth muscles are involuntary and non-striated.
  • Cardiac muscles are involuntary and striated.
  • Sarcomere is the functional unit of striated muscle.
  • Actin forms the thin filament.
  • Myosin forms the thick filament.
  • Calcium ions initiate the contractile mechanism.
  • ATP is required for the cross-bridge cycle.
  • Sliding filament theory explains muscle contraction.
